Abstract
In protected horticultural production, tomato late blight shows strong environmental
inducibility, with a short latent period, rapid risk accumulation, and a limited control
window, which challenges conventional post-event disease monitoring. To address this, a
tomato late blight risk perception and predictive control approach for protected production
is proposed, integrating deep temporal modeling of environmental factors, visual symptom
perception, and risk-driven greenhouse control to enable prospective assessment and
proactive intervention. Based on disease mechanisms and real greenhouse conditions, an
artificial intelligence (AI) framework covering perception, prediction, and regulation is
constructed, moving beyond reliance on visible symptoms alone. Long-term evolution
of key variables, including temperature, air humidity, leaf wetness, and light intensity,
is modeled using deep temporal networks, while early weak lesions and subtle texture
changes are captured by visual models. Cross-modal fusion in a unified risk space generates
continuous risk scores to drive greenhouse regulation. Experiments on a multimodal
dataset from a real greenhouse in Bayannur, Inner Mongolia, show that the proposed
method outperforms vision-based and environment-based baselines in recognition and
risk prediction. It achieves about 0.95 accuracy, 0.94 F1-score, and over 0.97 area under
the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), while providing more than 20 h of
early warning before disease onset. In environmental modeling, the deep temporal model
consistently surpasses threshold-based methods, logistic regression, and long short-term
memory/gated recurrent unit (LSTM/GRU) baselines in risk lead time, false alert rate, and
prediction stability.
Keywords: artificial intelligence in horticulture; early disease warning; greenhouse
microclimate; precision disease management; tomato late blight
1. Introduction
Tomato late blight caused by Phytophthora in f estans is one of the most destructive
diseases in protected tomato production, characterized by a short latent period, rapid
transmission, and explosive outbreaks, especially under greenhouse conditions with high
humidity and frequent condensation [1]. Without timely control, large-scale yield losses
or even complete crop failure may occur, threatening economic efficiency and production
stability [2]. Compared with open-field conditions, greenhouse environments are highly
controllable, where temperature, humidity, ventilation, and illumination can be regulated,
providing a foundation for disease pre-warning and proactive management [3]. However,

late blight control still mainly relies on experience-based pesticide application or periodic
manual inspection, lacking quantitative risk assessment and dynamic prediction [4]. Ex-
cessive dependence on experience often causes delayed intervention or pesticide overuse,
while actions taken after visible symptoms usually miss the optimal preventionwindow [5].
Therefore, developing an intelligent system capable of identifying disease risk before symp-
tom manifestation and interacting with greenhouse regulation is crucial for precise control,
reduced pesticide use, and improved intelligence in protected agriculture [6].
Traditional monitoring and prediction for tomato late blight in protected horticulture
generally follows three lines: manual scouting, environment rule-based risk indices, and
statistical/mechanistic modeling [ 7,8]. In commercial greenhouses, however, these ap-
proaches face practical constraints that limit their value for precision management. Manual
scouting is difficult to scale to dense canopies and multi-compartment facilities, and the
sparse sampling frequency often fails to capture rapid microclimate transitions (e.g., noc-
turnal humidity spikes or short-duration leaf wetness) that can drive infection, resulting
in delayed responses [9,10]. Environment threshold-based assessment transforms temper-
ature, relative humidity, or leaf wetness into fixed rules, but greenhouse microclimates
are highly heterogeneous and strongly affected by structure, ventilation strategy, crop
stage, and sensor placement. As a consequence, thresholds tuned in one facility or season
often transfer poorly, producing frequent false alarms or missed warnings and providing
limited guidance for operational regulation [11]. Statistical and mechanistic models aim to
formalize environment–disease relationships, yet they typically rely on simplified assump-
tions and struggle to represent nonlinear interactions and long-term cumulative effects
among coupled variables, which are common under protected cultivation [12,13]. Since
disease development is the result of prolonged exposure to joint stressors rather than a
single instantaneous condition [14], conventional models often cannot deliver stable early
warning signals that are actionable for fine-grained greenhouse control [15].
With the rise of smart agriculture infrastructure and vision-based phenotyping, CNN-
and Transformer-based recognition has achieved strong performance for late blight identi-
fication from RGB images [16,17], especially when symptoms are visually apparent [18].
Nevertheless, most studies still formulate the task as static classification or segmentation
on single images, which implicitly assumes that visible lesions are the primary trigger for
decision-making [11,19]. This post-event paradigm is inherently limited in protected horti-
culture, where the latent phase can span hours to days and where management requires
intervention before lesions become obvious [20]. More importantly, existing vision-only
pipelines rarely incorporate the evolving environmental predisposition and its cumula-
tive impact, and thus cannot explain risk escalation under symptom-free conditions or
support proactive regulation policies such as dehumidification scheduling, ventilation
timing, or irrigation adjustments [ 21,22]. Although multimodal fusion and time-series
modeling have been explored, most prior work remains focused on detection/diagnosis
rather than operational decision support, and systematic closed-loop solutions that align
environmental time-series, visual cues, and controllable actions are still scarce for tomato
late blight management [23,24]. Representative studies include ensemble diagnosis using
prescription-related data [25], real-time object detection pipelines [26], participatory system
modeling [27], and digital-twin-based pest management [28]; however, they typically lack
at least one of the following capabilities: (i) explicit modeling of long-horizon environ-
mental accumulation leading to infection [29], (ii) risk quantification under weak/early
visual symptoms [30], and (iii) a risk-to-control mapping that enables proactive greenhouse
regulation rather than post hoc reporting [31].
To address the critical gap between passive disease monitoring and active environmen-
tal regulation, this study proposes a proactive multimodal risk perception and predictive

1. A disease risk perception and predictive control framework for greenhouse tomato pro-
duction is proposed, integrating visual perception, environmental temporal modeling,

nonlinear long-term evolution of key variables such as temperature, humidity, and

improving the stability and reliability of early-stage risk assessment.

continuous risk scores to regulation decisions for proactive intervention in real
greenhouse scenarios.
2. Related Work
2.1. Limitations of Visual Perception in Early Disease Warning
With the rapid advancement of computer vision, deep learning has fundamentally
transformed crop disease recognition, enabling automatic feature extraction that bypasses
the reliance on handcrafted descriptors and expert knowledge [ 32,33]. Convolutional
Neural Networks (CNNs), utilizing local receptive fields and weight sharing, have demon-
strated strong discriminative capabilities in capturing leaf texture, color variations, and
lesion morphology [34]. These models have achieved high accuracy on standard datasets
across various crops, including tomato, apple, and wheat [35]. Specifically for tomato late
blight, end-to-end classification models based on RGB images of leaves or fruits have effec-
tively replaced manual inspection in many scenarios [36]. Recently, Vision Transformers
have further enhanced robustness against complex backgrounds and multi-scale lesions by
modeling global features via self-attention mechanisms [37], thereby alleviating the locality
bias of CNNs and improving generalization [38].
However, in the specific context of proactive greenhouse management, purely vision-
based approaches exhibit inherent limitations. High recognition accuracy is predominantly
achieved at middle to late infection stages when lesions are clearly visible [39], effectively
restricting these methods to a “post-event” recognition paradigm [40]. During the latent or
early infection stages, symptoms manifest only as subtle texture or color changes that are

difficult to discriminate, leading to high misclassification rates [41]. More critically, single-
modality visual modeling isolates the plant from its growth environment, ignoring the
cumulative environmental context prior to disease onset [42]. Consequently, these models
cannot reflect the dynamic risk evolution driven by environmental changes, limiting their
utility for early warning and proactive control before symptoms become irreversible.
2.2. Challenges in Environmental Risk Modeling and Mechanism Integration
Unlike open-field agriculture, greenhouse environments allow for the monitoring
and regulation of disease-inducing factors, emphasizing the influence of microclimatic
conditions on disease occurrence [ 43]. Sensor networks are widely deployed to collect
critical variables such as temperature, humidity, leaf wetness, and light intensity for risk
assessment [30]. For tomato late blight, epidemiological studies have confirmed that out-
breaks are strongly triggered by specific environmental patterns, particularly sustained
high humidity, leaf condensation, and suitable temperature windows [44]. Early manage-
ment strategies primarily relied on empirical or threshold-based models, which define
fixed rules for humidity or wetness duration to predict risk [45]. While simple to imple-
ment, these static threshold methods struggle to adapt to the dynamic heterogeneity of
different greenhouse structures and cultivars, resulting in unstable predictions and weak
generalization [46,47].
To address these rigidities, statistical and probabilistic models were introduced to
quantify environment–disease relationships using historical data [ 48,49]. Nevertheless,
environmental variables in greenhouses exhibit strong nonlinearity and coupling, and
disease occurrence is often the result of long-term accumulation rather than instantaneous
triggers, a complexity that conventional statistical models often fail to capture [ 50]. Al-
though mechanism-based models offer theoretical depth, they require detailed pathogen
growth and infection parameters that are notoriously difficult to obtain in real-time pro-
duction settings [51]. Therefore, modeling the complex, nonlinear, and time-dependent
relationship between environmental factors and disease risk without relying on strong
prior assumptions remains a significant challenge for effective early warning.
2.3. Multimodal Temporal Fusion and Closed-Loop Control
To overcome the limitations of single-source modalities, multimodal and tempo-
ral modeling has emerged as a critical trend in smart agriculture [ 52]. The integration
of visual data with environmental time series and meteorological records allows for a
more holistic capture of dynamic evolution processes, significantly enhancing prediction
capabilities [53]. In this paradigm, multimodal fusion compensates for the blind spots
of individual sensors, while deep temporal modeling captures long-term dependencies
and developmental trends [54]. Architectures such as LSTM and GRU have been exten-
sively applied to environmental prediction and crop growth modeling [ 55], and recent
Transformer-based variants have further improved long-horizon modeling through global
self-attention mechanisms [56]. These advanced methods have shown promise in diverse
tasks ranging from yield forecasting to disaster assessment [57].
However, a critical gap remains in the domain of disease management: most existing
studies treat environmental parameter prediction and disease recognition as separate,
isolated tasks, lacking a deep, unified integration of visual symptom perception and
environmental temporal modeling [58]. Particularly for environment-triggered diseases
like tomato late blight, the potential of linking continuous risk assessment directly to
greenhouse control systems (e.g., ventilation or dehumidification) remains insufficiently
explored [24]. Addressing this disconnect, this study proposes a system that integrates
visual information with environmental evolution patterns using multimodal temporal deep

3. Materials and Method
3.1. Data Collection
Data collection was conducted in a smart multi-span greenhouse under real pro-
duction conditions located in Bayannur, Inner Mongolia, China. To ensure the dataset
possessed sufficient environmental diversity and representativeness, the data acquisition
period spanned a complete production cycle from November 2023 to March 2024. This
five-month window captured the critical seasonal transition from winter to early spring, a
period characterized by pronounced diurnal temperature variations (often exceeding10 ◦C)
and frequent high-humidity events caused by condensation. These dynamic microclimatic
conditions readily satisfy the environmental thresholds for tomato late blight incubation
and outbreak, allowing the dataset to encompass a wide spectrum of environmental states
ranging from low-risk stable intervals to high-risk inductive periods. The experimental ob-
jects were greenhouse-grown tomato crops, monitored continuously through transplanting,
vegetative growth, flowering, and fruit setting phases, as summarized in Table 1. Tomato
leaf image data were collected using fixed high-resolution industrial RGB cameras de-
ployed between crop rows. The camera installation height was maintained approximately
at the canopy level, with shooting angles arranged nearly perpendicular to the leaf surface
to minimize occlusion. Image acquisition was automatically triggered at multiple key
intervals daily—morning, midday, and evening—to capture leaf appearance under diverse
natural illumination levels and physiological states. Synchronous recording of greenhouse
supplementary lighting and ventilation status ensured that the image data faithfully re-
flected the dynamic influence of environmental regulation on leaf texture and symptom
evolution. Consequently, the final image dataset explicitly represents the full continuum
of disease development, including healthy leaves, the challenging pre-symptomatic latent
stage identifiable only through environmental history, and various severities of visible late
blight lesions, as illustrated in Figure 1.

3.2. Data Preprocessing and Augmentation Strategy
In multimodal disease risk perception and prediction, data quality and synchroniza-
tion largely determine whether a model can learn meaningful early-risk patterns. In this
study, tomato leaf images and greenhouse environmental sensor time series differ sub-
stantially in acquisition mechanisms, noise characteristics, and sampling frequency. If
preprocessing is not handled carefully, modality imbalance and temporal misalignment can
dilute weak pre-symptomatic signals and reduce the reliability of risk prediction. Therefore,
before model training, the raw data were processed systematically to ensure distribution
consistency, temporal continuity, and representative learning samples.
Image preprocessing. Tomato leaf images were collected under natural greenhouse
conditions, and thus exhibit variations in shooting distance, viewpoint, background clutter,
and illumination. First, a leaf-centered region of interest was cropped to suppress irrelevant
background and encourage the visual model to focus on leaf texture and color cues associ-
ated with early infection. Cropping was performed using fixed-size windows centered on
the manually verified leaf area in each image, and images with insufficient leaf coverage
were removed [59]. Second, all cropped images were resized to a unified input resolution
to eliminate scale inconsistency across batches and to stabilize training [ 60]. Third, illu-
mination differences across time (e.g., cloudy versus sunny periods) were addressed by
standardizing pixel intensities using the mean and standard deviation computed from the
training set, so that images acquired at different times share comparable brightness and
contrast statistics [61]. This normalization improves robustness to lighting changes and
helps the model attend to subtle early-stage lesion patterns.
Environmental time-series preprocessing. Environmental variables (temperature, air
humidity, leaf wetness, and light intensity) were recorded continuously at fixed intervals,
but inevitably contain short-term noise spikes and occasional missing values due to sen-
sor precision limits, communication dropouts, or maintenance. To reduce the impact of
transient fluctuations that do not reflect real microclimate evolution, each environmental
channel was smoothed using a sliding-window moving average (with a small window cho-
sen to suppress high-frequency noise while preserving diurnal and multi-hour trends) [62].
Missing values were handled by time-continuity interpolation: when isolated gaps oc-
curred, linear interpolation between the nearest valid observations was applied; when
longer gaps occurred, the affected segments were excluded from training to avoid injecting
unreliable artificial patterns [63]. After denoising and gap handling, each environmental
channel was normalized (z-score) using training-set statistics to place variables with dif-

ferent physical units on a comparable scale, which benefits stable temporal modeling and
cross-modal fusion [64].
Temporal alignment across modalities. Image acquisition and environmental sensing
are not perfectly synchronized and typically differ in sampling frequency. To construct
unified multimodal samples, each image was paired with the temporally nearest environ-
mental record. When multiple environmental records fell within a short tolerance window
around an image timestamp, the closest record was selected; if no environmental record
was available within the tolerance due to missing sensor data, the corresponding image
sample was discarded. This alignment ensures that visual cues are associated with a con-
sistent snapshot of the contemporaneous microclimate, which is essential for subsequent
cross-modal risk modeling [65].
Augmentation and class-imbalance handling. Early-stage late blight symptoms are
subtle and appear less frequently than healthy samples, which can bias training toward
dominant classes. To improve generalization under realistic greenhouse variability and to
strengthen sensitivity to weak lesions, data augmentation was applied online during train-
ing, with stronger augmentation applied to early-stage samples. Augmentations included
random rotation [66], horizontal/vertical flipping [67], and mild brightness/contrast jitter
within bounded ranges that preserve disease-relevant texture [68]. This strategy increases
the effective diversity of early-stage images without changing their semantic disease char-
acteristics, alleviating class imbalance and improving robustness.
3.3. Proposed Method
3.3.1. Overall
At the model design level, a closed-loop pipeline consisting of visual perception,
environmental factor temporal modeling, cross-modal risk fusion, and predictive control
mapping is constructed. Preprocessed multimodal samples are fed into the system in a
time-aligned manner. At each time step t, the leaf image It and the corresponding envi-
ronmental vector Et ∈ Rd are synchronously input. First, the visual symptom perception
module takes It as input and extracts hierarchical representations through a deep visual
encoder, producing a visual feature vt = fvis(It). This feature emphasizes sensitivity to
weak lesions, early texture variations, and subtle color anomalies, thereby reflecting the
current latent pathological state of the crop. Second, the environmental factor modeling
module receives the past L-step environmental sequence {Et−L+1, . . ., Et} as input, and
a deep temporal network is employed to learn long-term dependencies and nonlinear
couplings among environmental variables, yielding a temporal environmental represen-
tation st = fenv(Et−L+1:t) that characterizes the evolution of risk triggered by sustained
environmental accumulation. Subsequently, the cross-modal risk fusion module maps vt
and st into a unified risk semantic space and performs joint modeling to obtain a fused
feature zt = ffus([vt, st]). A risk regression head further outputs a continuous risk score
rt = g(zt) ∈ [0, 1], where rt represents either the instantaneous infection probability or a
continuous measure of risk intensity, thus supporting prospective assessment during the
latent period. At the decision level, the predictive control interface mapsrt and its temporal
variation to greenhouse control actions at = π(rt, ∆rt), enabling the triggering or intensity
adjustment of ventilation, dehumidification, and supplementary lighting. Through the
stepwise coupling of these modules, the system completes the full process from multi-
modal representation learning to continuous risk evaluation and control linkage within a
unified framework, realizing a deployable predictive control loop for greenhouse tomato
late blight.

3.3.2. Environmental Factor Modeling Module Based on Deep Temporal Networks
The environmental factor modeling module based on deep temporal networks is de-
signed to extract latent risk representations closely associated with tomato late blight from
continuous environmental observations. The core principle is not to judge environmental
states at a single moment, but to systematically model the cumulative effects and nonlinear
temporal evolution of environmental predisposing factors.
As shown in Figure 2, time-aligned greenhouse sensor data are first organized into a
length-L temporal sequence Et−L+1:t = {Et−L+1, . . ., Et}, where each environmental vector
comprises multiple variables, including air temperature, air relative humidity, leaf wetness,
and light intensity. This sequence is fed into a deep temporal encoding network for feature
extraction. The encoder is constructed by stacking multiple temporal units, in which
gated mechanisms selectively memorize and update historical information, allowing short-
term random fluctuations to be distinguished from long-term stable trends and thereby
forming high-dimensional temporal representations of environmental factors. To further
enhance adaptive modeling of the relative importance of different time segments, a routing-
based feature selection structure is introduced after temporal encoding. As illustrated,
environmental temporal features are treated as a set of temporal tokens, concatenated and
fed into a routing MLP to model the risk relevance of each token. A probability distribution
is obtained through softmax normalization, characterizing the contribution of each time
segment to the current risk assessment. Based on this distribution, highly relevant tokens
are emphasized in the latent space, while tokens with lower contributions are suppressed
or pruned, enabling focused attention on critical environmental stages. Mathematically,
this process constructs a weighted environmental representation st = ∑i αihi, where hi
represents the temporal features and αi denotes the normalized attention weights derived
from the routing mapping. To improve readability, the detailed mathematical formulations
of the routing function and the theoretical stability bounds ensuring temporal consistency
under sensory noise are provided in Appendix A. This representation serves as the final
output of the environmental factor modeling module and is passed to the subsequent
cross-modal fusion stage. Such a design enables explicit modeling of which temporal
segments of environmental conditions are most critical to the current disease risk, avoiding
information dilution caused by simple averaging or fixed-window aggregation. From a
task perspective, this module enhances sensitivity to key predisposing patterns such as
sustained high humidity and repeated condensation, improves adaptability to diverse
greenhouse operating conditions and seasonal variations, and produces smoother, more
stable, and forward-looking risk assessments, thereby providing a reliable environmental
risk semantic basis for subsequent fusion with visual symptom information and predictive
control decision-making.

3.3.4. Cross-Modal Risk Fusion and Predictive Control Strategy
The cross-modal risk fusion and predictive control strategy takes high-level semantic

4.1. Experimental Configuration
4.1.1. Implementation Details
The experimental hardware infrastructure was constructed using a high-performance
deep learning workstation coupled with an industrial-grade greenhouse sensing network.
The central computing server operated on the Ubuntu 22.04 LTS system, equipped with
an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU (2.90 GHz, 16 cores) and 128 GB of DDR4 ECC RAM to
handle large-scale multimodal data preprocessing. To satisfy the intensive computational
requirements of the proposed Transformer-based visual encoder and temporal networks,
model training and inference were accelerated by two NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs
(24 GB GDDR6X VRAM each), interconnected via NVLink to support high-throughput
parallel computing. On the greenhouse side in Bayannur, data acquisition relied on an
RS485 Modbus IoT system. Environmental variables, including air temperature and relative
humidity, were monitored using high-precision SHT30-based sensors (Sensirion, Stäfa,
Switzerland, accuracy ± 0.2 ◦C, ±2%), while leaf wetness was recorded via dielectric
RY-CYM/485 sensors (Handan Yunnong Intelligent Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd.,
Handan, China) calibrated to a 0–100% range. Visual data were captured by Hikvision DS-
2CD series high-definition IP network cameras (Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology
Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) installed at canopy height, configured to acquire images at
a resolution of 2560 × 1440 pixels, ensuring high-fidelity input for the visual symptom
perception module.
From the software perspective, the proposed framework was implemented using
Python 3.9 and the PyTorch 2.0.1 deep learning library, accelerated by CUDA 11.8 and
cuDNN 8.5 backends. To ensure experimental reproducibility, all random seeds for Python,
NumPy, and PyTorch were fixed at42. Image preprocessing and augmentation, including
random cropping and illumination normalization, were executed using OpenCV 4.7 and the
Albumentations 1.3 library, while environmental time-series data cleaning and alignment
were handled via Pandas 2.0. The communication interface between the risk prediction
model and the greenhouse control system was deployed as a RESTful API using the
FastAPI framework, allowing for real-time risk score transmission and control instruction
dispatch. This modular software architecture ensured the stability of the training process
and facilitated the seamless integration of the predictive control strategy into the existing
greenhouse management system.
During the model training phase, a rigorous experimental design was implemented
to address the inherent temporal dependencies of the collected data. Unlike conventional
random shuffling, which may introduce look-ahead bias in time-series forecasting, the
dataset was partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets using
a strict chronological splitting strategy. To explicitly prevent temporal information leakage
across these data boundaries caused by the historical environmental window a temporal
buffer interval strictly equal to L = 1440, corresponding to exactly 24 h of minute-level
sensor observations, was enforced between the adjacent subsets. This gap guarantees
that no single continuous sliding window spans multiple partitions, ensuring that the
model is trained exclusively on historical environmental trajectories and evaluated on
truly unseen future data, thereby faithfully simulating real-world deployment scenarios.
Visual inputs were standardized to 224 × 224 pixels to feed the hierarchical visual encoder,
which was configured with depths of {2, 2, 6, 2} to capture multi-scale features. Cross-
modal embeddings were projected into a 256-dimensional space and fused via multi-head
attention (h = 8). The network was optimized using AdamW (weight decay 0.05, batch size
32, initial learning rate 1 × 10−4 with Cosine Annealing). The training spanned 200 epochs
with early stopping (patience 20) and a loss balancing coefficient λ = 0.6. Furthermore,

4.4. Comprehensive Evaluation of Greenhouse Control Linkage
The purpose of this experiment is to verify whether the proposed risk perception
model not only achieves advantages at the prediction level, but also generates quantifiable
and sustainable disease control effects through linkage with greenhouse control strate-
gies under real production conditions. Accordingly, two management modes, namely
risk-independent control and risk-driven linkage control, are compared under identical
cultivation settings. The evaluation focuses on multiple dimensions, including disease
outcomes, environmental predisposing states, production inputs, and outputs. Rather than
assessing isolated indicators, this experiment examines whether predicted risk information
is effectively translated into targeted environmental regulation and whether such regula-
tion forms positive synergies among disease suppression, pesticide reduction, and yield
improvement, thereby validating the practical value of the prediction–control closed loop
in protected agriculture.
As shown in Table 4 and Figure 7, the introduction of risk-driven control linkage leads
to substantial reductions in both disease incidence and severe symptom rates, indicating
that risk-based environmental interventions effectively weaken disease development con-
ditions before the diffusion stage. Meanwhile, significant decreases are observed in mean
relative humidity, duration of high-humidity exposure, and leaf wetness duration, suggest-
ing that the control strategy effectively redistributes high-risk environmental states over
time and reduces cumulative predisposing effects. The concurrent reduction in high-risk
duration and event frequency further demonstrates that risk-driven regulation not only
lowers risk magnitude but also improves its temporal stability, thereby reducing disease
triggering probability. From an input–output perspective, linked control substantially
reduces pesticide application frequency and total dosage while increasing marketable yield
and lowering disease-induced yield loss. This outcome reflects that targeted environmen-
tal regulation can partially substitute chemical interventions. Although the frequency
of environmental control actions increases under linked control, these actions are more
fine-grained and targeted. From a control-theoretic viewpoint, such high-frequency, low-
amplitude adjustments help maintain system states within safe operating ranges and avoid
delayed, large-scale corrective interventions. Overall, these results validate the rationality
of using predicted risk as a control input from both mathematical behavior and system
response perspectives, demonstrating that a stable closed loop can be formed in complex
greenhouse systems and that disease management can be shifted from experience-driven
practices to proactive regulation based on risk evolution mechanisms.

4.5. Ablation Study of the Proposed System Components
The ablation study is designed to systematically verify the practical contributions and
synergistic effects of each functional module within the proposed framework, and to clarify
the functional roles of different components in disease recognition accuracy and early risk
warning capability. By sequentially removing key components, including environmental
temporal modeling, visual symptom perception, cross-modal fusion, and risk-to-control
mapping, while keeping the dataset and training strategy unchanged, performance vari-
ations can be analyzed from a structural perspective. This design enables an explicit
examination of whether the overall performance improvement relies on a single dominant
module or arises from complementary interactions among multiple modules. In particular,
the risk lead time metric provides an intuitive measure of how different modules contribute

to modeling risk evolution during the latent stage of disease development, thereby offering
structural evidence for the rationality of the proposed model design.
As shown in Table 5 and Figure 8, the full model achieves the best performance across
all metrics, demonstrating the overall advantage of the multimodal temporal modeling and
risk fusion framework. When the environmental temporal modeling module is removed,
the most pronounced degradation is observed in risk lead time, whereas classification-
related metrics exhibit relatively limited changes. This observation indicates that the
environmental module primarily the modeling of historical risk accumulation effects,
and its absence causes the model to degenerate into a short-sighted assessment based
mainly on the current state, thereby limiting its ability to extrapolate risk during the
latent stage. When the visual symptom perception module is removed, the risk lead
time remains relatively high, but accuracy and F1-score decrease noticeably, suggesting
that environmental predisposing factors provide stable prospective information, while
the visual module acts as an instantaneous constraint on risk estimation that improves
discrimination of the current disease state. Replacing cross-modal fusion with simple
late-stage score averaging results in consistent performance degradation across all metrics,
highlighting the importance of dynamic weighting of heterogeneous information sources
in the feature space rather than treating them as independent or equally weighted signals.
Notably, removing the risk-to-control mapping does not affect prediction metrics, which is
theoretically expected because this module does not participate in risk estimation itself but
contributes at the system level through closed-loop regulation, with its effects primarily
reflected in long-term environmental feedback and disease outcomes. Overall, the ablation
results confirm from both structural and functional perspectives that the modular design of
the proposed model is necessary and complementary, and that balanced improvements
in accuracy, stability, and foresight can only be achieved through the joint operation of
all components.

5. Discussion
5.1. Comparison with Existing Literature and Hypothesis Validation
The experimental outcomes of this study strongly support our initial hypothesis:
explicitly integrating long-horizon environmental accumulation with instantaneous visual
symptom perception significantly extends the early warning lead time and enhances the
stability of risk assessment compared to single-modality approaches. When contrasted with
recent vision-only disease recognition models [37,39], which predominantly excel during
the post-event symptomatic phase, our multimodal framework successfully mitigates the
high misclassification rates typically observed during latent or early infection stages [41].
This confirms that isolating the plant from its dynamic environmental context severely limits
the potential for proactive management [42]. Furthermore, our deep temporal modeling of
environmental factors demonstrates clear superiority over traditional empirical threshold-
based methods [45] and conventional statistical models [50]. While previous studies have
highlighted the instability and weak generalization of static rules under heterogeneous
greenhouse conditions [ 46,47], our continuous risk semantic space effectively captures
the nonlinear, cumulative nature of predisposing factors—such as sustained humidity
and condensation—without relying on rigid prior assumptions. Most critically, whereas
recent advances in smart agriculture have explored multimodal temporal fusion [53,54],
the majority of these efforts have historically treated environmental prediction and disease
diagnosis as disconnected diagnostic tasks [ 24,58]. Our findings validate that linking a
continuous, unified risk assessment directly to a predictive control policy successfully
bridges this critical gap, advancing the field from isolated monitoring toward a truly
proactive, closed-loop greenhouse management paradigm.
5.2. Mechanistic Interpretation of Performance Gains
The results demonstrate that integrating environmental temporal modeling with early
visual perception yields not only higher recognition metrics but also a more meaningful
improvement in forward-looking risk prediction, particularly in risk lead time and sta-
bility. This advantage is consistent with the disease mechanism of tomato late blight in
protected cultivation: infection is typically enabled by sustained predisposing conditions
(e.g., prolonged high relative humidity, repeated condensation events, and persistent leaf
wetness), followed by a latent phase during which pathogen development progresses
before clear lesions become visually apparent. Vision-only baselines therefore tend to
peak in confidence only after macroscopic symptoms emerge, which inherently constrains
lead time; moreover, early-stage leaf appearance is easily confounded by illumination
variation, occlusion, and background clutter, reducing reliability. In contrast, the proposed
deep temporal environmental module captures long-horizon, nonlinear accumulation of

predisposing exposure by modeling coupled variables such as temperature, humidity, leaf
wetness, and light intensity as evolving trajectories rather than isolated instantaneous states.
This design aligns with the cumulative nature of infection risk and enables the risk score to
rise earlier than symptom-driven detectors, explaining the observed gain in early-warning
lead time.
The stability improvement arises from the way the model distinguishes persistent
risk evolution from short-lived microclimate fluctuations. Threshold-based and simpler
statistical models respond sharply to transient excursions (e.g., brief humidity spikes or
sensor noise), which often yields oscillatory alerts and poor operational trust. By learning
temporal context, the proposed environmental encoder produces smoother risk trajectories
that reflect sustained exposure, thereby reducing false alerts and improving prediction
stability. Meanwhile, the visual branch provides complementary evidence that helps
calibrate the risk estimate when the environment is temporarily favorable but plants
remain healthy, and it strengthens risk confidence when weak lesions and subtle texture
changes begin to appear. The cross-modal fusion in a unified risk space reconciles these two
forms of evidence—environmental predisposition and incipient visual manifestations—so
the overall risk estimate becomes less sensitive to modality-specific uncertainty and more
robust under real greenhouse variability. Ablation results support this interpretation by
showing that removing either the temporal environmental modeling or the visual cues
degrades lead time, stability, and/or false alert control, indicating that the gain is not a
marginal ensemble effect but a mechanistically coherent complementarity.
Despite these strengths, the approach also has practical limitations that should be
acknowledged. The reliability of risk prediction depends on the continuity and quality of
sensor measurements and on stable image acquisition; sensor drift, missing data, camera
viewpoint changes, or severe canopy occlusion can degrade performance. In addition, the
current risk-to-control linkage is designed to be engineering-feasible and interpretable,
but it may not yet fully optimize multi-objective trade-offs (disease suppression versus
energy cost, crop growth stage, cultivar resistance, and concurrent stresses). Finally, gener-
alization across regions, seasons, and greenhouse designs requires broader validation and,
potentially, domain adaptation and uncertainty calibration to provide confidence-aware
decision support.
5.3. Implications for Growers and Greenhouse Management Systems
From the standpoint of growers and greenhouse operators, the primary value of the
proposed framework is that it converts heterogeneous sensing streams into a continuous,
interpretable risk signal that can be acted upon before visible symptoms emerge. In
practice, late blight outbreaks are rarely instantaneous; they are often preceded by periods
of sustained humidity and leaf wetness that can be mitigated through timely ventilation,
dehumidification, heating, or irrigation scheduling. By providing early warning tied to
risk evolution rather than to visible lesions, the system supports earlier, smaller, and
more targeted operational adjustments that reduce the formation of disease-favorable
microclimates at the source. This capability is especially important in high-density, high-
investment protected tomato production, where delayed interventions can lead to rapid
spread, higher chemical dependency, and yield/quality penalties.
The framework is also compatible with existing greenhouse automation workflows
because the risk score can be directly integrated as a supervisory signal for control logic.
Instead of relying on fixed thresholds that often require repeated manual retuning across
facilities and seasons, the learned risk trajectory provides a more adaptive indicator for
when to intensify ventilation or dehumidification and when to relax actions to avoid
unnecessary energy expenditure. The visual module further improves operational trust

variables have recently improved. At the production level, this coupling of prediction
and regulation offers a practical pathway toward reduced pesticide frequency and dosage
while maintaining or improving marketable yield, supporting the broader goals of low-
chemical and sustainable protected horticulture. Looking ahead, embedding the approach
into decision-support interfaces with uncertainty reporting and extending it to additional
sensors and other environment-induced diseases can further increase adoption value and
scalability for intelligent greenhouse management.
5.4. Generalizability and Domain Adaptation
Given that the proposed multimodal framework involves a relatively complex archi-
tecture, the risk of overfitting on the moderately sized dataset (approximately 18,600 images
and corresponding environmental series from a single greenhouse) was strictly mitigated
through comprehensive regularization strategies. In addition to early stopping and weight
decay, the training pipeline incorporated dynamic online data augmentation for the visual
inputs, Dropout and DropPath (stochastic depth) regularization within the hierarchical vi-
sual encoder and Transformer fusion blocks, and feature-level masking during cross-modal
attention. These mechanisms collectively force the network to learn robust, generalized
representations rather than memorizing specific training samples. The efficacy of these
strategies is evidenced by the 5-fold time-series cross-validation results, where the perfor-
mance variance across different temporal folds remained remarkably low. This minimal
fluctuation confirms that the model achieves stable generalization within the target domain,
effectively avoiding overfitting to specific short-term microclimatic anomalies or localized
visual artifacts.
Although the multimodal predictive control system demonstrates significant internal
efficacy and stability, the current model was trained and validated using data from one
continuous winter-to-spring production cycle in Northern China. While the fundamental
biological mechanisms driving tomato late blight—specifically its strict dependence on
prolonged high humidity, leaf condensation, and specific temperature windows—are
universal across different geographic regions, the raw distribution and baseline fluctuations
of environmental sensor data are inherently influenced by local external climates and
specific greenhouse structural characteristics. Consequently, if the system were directly
deployed in a facility with a markedly different climate regime, such as a tropical or
Mediterranean environment, the baseline microclimate dynamics could shift, potentially
leading to suboptimal risk threshold triggering or altered early warning lead times. To
ensure robust performance in entirely new environments, the system is designed to undergo
a lightweight recalibration process rather than complete retraining from scratch. Because
the visual perception module learns morphological and textural lesion features that remain
highly consistent across different regions, domain adaptation would primarily focus on the
environmental temporal module. By collecting local microclimate data for a brief initial
period without requiring manual disease annotations, unsupervised domain adaptation
techniques or statistical re-normalization can be applied to align the new environmental
feature distribution with the source domain. This localized recalibration strategy would
effectively adapt the predictive control policy to the specific thermodynamic behavior and
ventilation efficiency of the new greenhouse, thereby ensuring that proactive interventions
remain both timely and resource-efficient across diverse agricultural settings.

5.5. Limitations and Future Work
Although the proposed tomato late blight risk perception and predictive control sys-
tem demonstrates strong early warning capability and effective control performance in
real greenhouse environments, several aspects remain to be further improved. First, the
approach relies on multisource sensors and fixed-view imaging devices, and its perfor-
mance is influenced by sensor deployment density, device stability, and data continuity.
Under extreme environmental fluctuations or sensor malfunction, risk estimation accu-
racy may be affected. Second, the current control strategy primarily maps risk scores and
their temporal trends to control actions through rule-based mechanisms, without fully
accounting for differences in crop growth stages, varietal resistance, or concurrent multiple
diseases, which may limit the granularity of control decisions. Future work should include
long-term validation across larger-scale and multi-regional greenhouse environments to
assess generalization under diverse climatic conditions and cultivation practices. Incorpo-
rating crop physiological indicators, growth stage information, and historical management
records could further enhance the completeness of the risk perception framework. In
addition, adaptive control strategies based on reinforcement learning or optimal control
could be explored to dynamically optimize decisions under multi-objective constraints.
Extending the framework to multi-disease and multi-crop scenarios and integrating it
with existing greenhouse management platforms are also expected to further increase its
practical applicability and deployment potential in real agricultural production.
6. Conclusions
This study successfully established and validated a proactive multimodal risk per-
ception and predictive control framework for managing tomato late blight in greenhouse
environments, directly addressing the limitations of passive, post-event disease monitor-
ing. By explicitly integrating the long-horizon nonlinear accumulation of environmental
predisposing factors—such as temperature, humidity, and leaf wetness—with early-stage
visual symptom representations, the proposed system constructs a unified semantic risk
space. This cross-modal synergy effectively leverages environmental trends to validate
subtle visual anomalies, generating a highly stable pre-symptomatic risk signal. Experi-
mental evaluations objectively confirmed our initial hypothesis, demonstrating that the
multimodal approach achieves robust early warning capabilities with an accuracy of 0.95,
an F1-score of 0.94, an AUC of 0.98, and a critical risk lead time exceeding 20 h. Crucially,
the framework successfully closes the conceptual loop established in the Introduction
by translating these continuous risk scores into an actionable, differentiable mapping for
automated greenhouse regulation. Field control trials provided quantitative evidence that
this risk-driven predictive control strategy significantly mitigates disease incidence and
severity, reduces the frequency and dosage of chemical pesticide applications, and main-
tains marketable yield. Ultimately, this research shifts the paradigm of greenhouse disease
management from reactive treatment to preemptive environmental modulation, provid-
ing a highly scalable and scientifically grounded foundation for the intelligent control of
environment-induced agricultural pathologies.
